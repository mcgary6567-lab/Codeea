How to Find Business Leads on Google Maps

What to search, what to write down, where the emails are hiding, and how to tell a real, active business from a listing nobody has touched in years.

By Ihsan · November 17, 2026 · 9 min read

Short answer: search Google Maps for a niche in a city ("plumbers in Denver"), save each listing's name, phone, website and rating, then open the website to find an email. Keep only businesses that look active, like recent reviews and owner replies, and contact each one with a short, personal message about something you noticed.

If you sell anything to local businesses, whether that's websites, SEO, ads, bookkeeping or software, Google Maps is probably the best free lead list you'll ever find. Business owners put their own details there because they want to be found. Most of them keep it up to date.

The catch is that it's slow to do well, and there's a lot of dead weight in there. Here's how I'd do it.

Step 1: Pick a niche and a city

Start with who actually pays for what you sell. A web designer might go after dentists, roofers or law firms. Someone selling review management might pick restaurants or salons. Be specific. "Businesses in Chicago" isn't a list, it's a phone book.

Then search the way a customer would: "roofers in Phoenix", "family dentist Austin", "Italian restaurant Leeds". A few tips:

  • Drag the map or zoom in on one neighbourhood at a time. Google Maps only shows a limited set of results for each view, so moving the map brings up businesses you wouldn't see otherwise.
  • Try the nearby towns too. Smaller towns get far fewer cold emails than big cities.
  • Use different words for the same thing ("dentist", "dental clinic", "orthodontist"). Each one surfaces different listings.

Step 2: Save the right details

Open each listing and copy what's useful into a spreadsheet. This is what I'd record:

Business nameObvious, but also how you'll spot duplicates later
PhoneFor calls, and to merge repeats
WebsiteWhere you'll find the email, and often your pitch
City / addressTime zone, and personalising the message
Rating and review countTells you how established they are
Newest review dateThe quickest sign the business is alive
EmailFilled in during step 3
NotesWhat you'll mention when you reach out

Step 3: Find the email address

Google Maps almost never shows an email. You'll find it on the business website. Check, in this order:

  1. The contact page
  2. The footer at the bottom of the home page
  3. The about or team page, which is where owner names and direct emails tend to be
  4. Any "book now" or quote form page

A generic address like info@ is fine to start with. If you can find the owner's name on the about page, use it in your greeting. That alone makes a cold email feel a lot less cold.

One more check before you send: make sure the domain can actually receive mail. Sites get rebuilt and old addresses stop working, and every bounce counts against your sending account.

Step 4: Keep only active businesses

This is the step most people skip, and it's why their lists feel useless. A lot of Maps listings belong to businesses that closed, moved or simply stopped caring. Emailing them wastes your sends and hurts your reply rate.

Signs a business is active:

  • Recent reviews, ideally in the last month or two
  • The owner replies to reviews. This is the best sign there is, because it means someone is reading
  • The listing is claimed and has current opening hours
  • The website loads, and has online booking, chat or a contact form

If a listing has none of these, move on. You'll get better results from 50 businesses that are clearly open than from 300 you're not sure about.

The phone number deserves its own check if you plan to call. Numbers get disconnected and listings don't always catch up.

Step 5: Find a reason to reach out

"I help businesses grow" gets deleted. "I noticed your site doesn't load properly on a phone" gets read. While you're on the website, look for something specific:

  • No website at all (the listing has no link)
  • A site that isn't mobile-friendly
  • No HTTPS, so browsers show "Not secure"
  • A slow site, or a broken contact form
  • Good reviews but no replies from the owner

Write it in your notes column. That one line becomes the opening of your email.

Step 6: Reach out the right way

Keep it short: who you are, what you noticed, one sentence on how you'd fix it, and a simple question. Then:

  • Send from your own inbox, a few a day at first. Blasting a hundred emails from a fresh Gmail on day one is how accounts get flagged.
  • Send during their working hours, in their time zone.
  • Send one polite follow-up, not five.
  • Include your postal address and an easy way to opt out, and honour opt-outs. In the US that's what the FTC's CAN-SPAM guide asks for. The UK, EU, Canada and Australia have stricter rules, so check before you email leads there.

Doing it by hand vs. with a tool

Everything above works by hand. I'd honestly suggest doing it manually for your first 20 or 30 leads, because you'll learn what a good lead in your niche looks like.

But it doesn't scale. Every lead means opening the listing, then the website, then hunting for an email, then checking whether the business is even still trading. Multiply that by a few hundred and it becomes your whole week.

Common mistakes

  • Emailing every listing. Without step 4 you're paying for dead leads with your sender reputation.
  • Not merging duplicates. Chains and businesses with several listings end up getting the same email twice. Match on website and phone.
  • Generic messages. If your email could be sent to any business, it reads like spam.
  • Going too fast. A new email account needs a few weeks of small daily volumes before it can send more.
